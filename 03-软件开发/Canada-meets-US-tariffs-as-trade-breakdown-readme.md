The breakdown of US-Canada trade negotiations has sent shockwaves through the global economy. Prime Minister Mark Carney announced that Canada will impose new tariffs on US goods, with a promise of reciprocal actions in response to unfair last-minute changes proposed by the United States. This development comes on the heels of hours of intense NEGOTIATIONS between the two sides and significant concerns that Canada's objectives for its citizens have not been met.

The US has threatened to impose tariffs on nearly $20bn worth of Canadian goods, including wine, dairy, cement, clothing, and hockey equipment. These new tariffs will apply to approximately 5% of Canada's exports and will be in addition to existing tariffs already imposed by the US on Canadian steel and aluminium, as well as autos and lumber.

Canada’s business community has expressed grave concerns over the impact of these new measures. The Canadian Chamber of Commerce called the tariffs a 'body blow to North American competitiveness'. For small Canadian exporters operating on tight margins, this means looking at orders, payroll, and employees to determine what can still be afforded. Canadians have told polls that they remain willing to support trade negotiations but are deeply concerned about the unfair terms presented by the United States.

The breakdown in trade talks marks a significant shift in the tone of US-Canada relations, moving from optimism about reaching a beneficial trade deal within the past few days to the current situation. The US and Canadian officials were reportedly discussing a deal that would reduce US tariffs on Canadian steel and aluminium from 50% to 25%, and on Canadian autos from 25% to 15%. Carney had asked Canadian provinces to restore US alcohol to store shelves as part of the agreement.

This shift has put the US-Canada relationship in uncharted territory, testing Canada's premiership. The two countries have one of the world's most deeply economically integrated trading relationships, with Canada accounting for approximately 70% of its exports to the US. However, tensions between the two major trading partners have been simmering since Trump returned to office last year and unleashed a wide-ranging global programme of tariffs.

The new US tariffs will be applied in addition to existing measures on Canadian steel and aluminium, autos, and lumber, affecting businesses across a range of sectors including wine, dairy, cement, clothing, and hockey equipment. The impact of these tariffs is expected to be felt not only by Canadian exports but also by businesses and stakeholders within both countries.

The new US deadlines for imposing these additional tariffs are creating uncertainty for global supply chains, further complicating the already complex landscape. As tensions rise, the economic implications of this trade breakdown will continue to unfold, setting the stage for further challenges in international relations and commerce.
